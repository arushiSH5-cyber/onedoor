# OneDoor — 3-minute pitch script (follows the 5 slides)

Total ≈ 3:00 at a calm pace (~140 words/minute). Slide file: `OneDoor_Pitch.pptx`. The same text is in each slide's speaker notes.

---

## Slide 1 — Title (0:00–0:20)

> Hi, I'm Arushi Nirmal, and this is OneDoor — for Brief 11, unclaimed energy assistance in New York City.
> Since January, every NYC household below area median income qualifies for a Con Edison discount. In the first month, 775 people enrolled in the new income pathway — out of almost 600,000.
> OneDoor is one five-minute check, by web or by text in seven languages, that gets eligible households their discount — even when the bill is in someone else's name.

## Slide 2 — Impact (0:20–0:55)

> Meet Rosa. She's a home health aide in the Bronx. Her mother gets SSI, but the bill is in Rosa's name — so no agency ever flagged them.
> Using Census data and the utility's own numbers, we estimate about 521,000 NYC households like Rosa's are eligible but not enrolled — concentrated in Flushing, Fordham, Soundview, Mott Haven and Jamaica.
> If OneDoor enrolls just 20% of that gap — 100,000 households — that's $50 million a year in discounts actually reaching people, and about 1,600 fewer shutoffs.
> And because renewal runs on data instead of paperwork, we expect 90% to still be enrolled after 18 months, instead of around 60%.

## Slide 3 — Innovation (0:55–1:35)

> Today there are two programs with two doors, and automatic enrollment only works for three benefits — matched to the account holder's name. That's exactly why Rosa is invisible.
> OneDoor matches homes, not names. Our screener asks about everyone in the home. If anyone gets SNAP, Medicaid, SSI or public housing, the household qualifies — and we flag it as a Household Link match.
> Then, with one checkbox — or one "YES" by text — the household consents to let the state match its service address against benefit records it already holds. That turns a one-time screening into near-automatic enrollment and renewal.
> The eligibility decision itself is a deterministic rules engine built from the official DPS and Con Edison rules. AI only helps fill in the form from free text — it never decides.

## Slide 4 — Sustainability (1:35–2:15)

> Discounts are paid by other ratepayers, so we built the ledger regulators need.
> For families, OneDoor is free and saves $33 to $190 a month.
> For everyone else: at 100,000 new enrollees, the net cost is about $1.68 a month per residential ratepayer. Fewer write-offs, collections and shutoffs offset about 7% of that. We show that honestly — the discount is a deliberate transfer, and OneDoor's job is to deliver it at the lowest admin cost.
> It runs on a DPS-designated matching hub and the utility's existing EAP team, with community boards, tenant associations and libraries doing outreach. And enrolled homes become the on-ramp to free efficiency and heat-pump programs.

## Slide 5 — Feasibility & Scalability (2:15–3:00)

> This works today. You're looking at Rosa's real result: EAP, Household Link match, $33.47 a month, and a consent code.
> It's tested — 39 unit tests plus end-to-end tests in English and Spanish — and built on real data: ACS 2024 and the official program rules.
> The legal pathway uses authority that already exists: the PSC's affordability proceeding, Case 14-M-0565, and the data channel HEAP already uses. Where federal rules require consent, OneDoor collects it.
> The UK pays 96% of its Warm Home Discount automatically; Brazil's Law 14,203 enrolls families straight from its social registry. Our playbook shows any place what it needs: a registry, an address key, a mandate and a data-sharing gateway.
> Our ask: a pilot partner at DPS or Con Edison and an anonymized address-match test file — starting with 5,000 homes in the Bronx. Thank you.
